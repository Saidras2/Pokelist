Route A5 vs Route A8 — flush magnetic lid

Same case, same self-assembly, same magnetic lid. One number differs — the wall thickness — and it decides the magnets, the size, the cost and how well the case survives a drop. Both are a true rectangular prism: flush lid, flush base, nothing protruding.

The whole case is four side sheets, one top sheet and one bottom sheet. That is it — six acrylic parts, no brackets, no pins, no extra acrylic pieces.

The joint shapes — the corner fingers, the base plate's corner tabs, the magnet pockets — are profiles cut into those six sheets. They are not additional parts. Non-acrylic items: 8 magnets, 6 foam pads, and adhesive for the foam.

01The two routes

Route A5

5 mm walls · slot pockets

Thin, glassy, cheaper. The magnet pocket is a 3 × 8 mm slot cut flush to the wall's inner face, so all 2 mm of acrylic sits on the outside where it matters. The floor sits one band up and is boxed in by the corner joinery — nothing sticks out underneath.

Route A8 Drop-safe pick

8 mm walls · round pockets

Chunky, stronger, about $2.50 more. Ordinary round pockets with 2 mm of acrylic on both sides of the magnet, and roughly 2.5× the impact energy before it cracks. Same captive floor, same flat underside.

03Why the wall thickness decides everything

A flush lid is a plain plate resting on the walls' rim, so the magnets have to sit vertically in the rim — and the rim is only as wide as the wall is thick.

A5 gets away with 5 mm by cutting the pocket as a slot flush with the wall's inner face, so the acrylic is all on the outside. The trade is that the slot is open on the inside — hidden by the booster box, but it is a notch in the inner top edge. A8 just uses ordinary round pockets.

04The base joint, explained simply

The four walls lock together at the corners with interlocking fingers, which makes a stack of little alternating blocks up each corner. Leave one block out of the stack, and the base plate has a tab that slots into the gap — so it is sandwiched between the blocks above and below.

That is the whole thing. No hook, no keyhole, no magnet, no glue.

Step by step

  1. The corners interlock. The walls do not butt together — at each corner wall A owns one 5 mm slice, wall B the next, alternating all the way up. That makes each corner a stack of little blocks.
  2. Leave one block out. Take the second block from the bottom out of the stack. You now have a rectangular gap exactly one block tall, with a block underneath it and a block above it.
  3. The base plate has a tab. At each of its four corners it has a little square tab sticking out, exactly block-sized. Slide the base in and each tab sits in one of those gaps.
  4. Now it cannot move. The block below stops it dropping, the block above stops it rising, and the walls' bodies stop it sliding.

Why the underside is flat

The walls do not stop at the gap. They carry on down past it, all the way to the floor. So the bottom of the case is the walls' own bottom edge — a continuous rim, one wall-thickness wide, all the way round, with nothing protruding.

The base ends up recessed one band inside. You cannot see that from outside, and it lifts the booster box another 5–8 mm off the ground.

05The base joint — enlarged section

Why it moved

The first version hooked the base from underneath. A hook head has to be trapped below the plate to work — which means it sticks out below the plate, and the case stands on four little feet. That was the fault.

So the floor moved one finger band up, inside the tube. The walls now run all the way down to the ground, and the case sits on their bottom edge — a continuous rim, all the way round.

06Why the walls slide the way they do

This is the bit that makes the corners and the base work in the same motion. I tested both options:

Wall motionFirst cornerSecond corner
Slide along the wall's own lengthmeshesjams solid part-way
Slide across the wall's own facemeshesmeshes at the same moment

A wall sliding along its length runs its material into the far wall's fingers part-way through the travel — it cannot reach the second corner. Sliding across its own face has no sweep collision at all, so both corners click home in one push. That same push drives the wall’s corner fingers past the base plate’s tabs. The whole assembly is two pushes.

07Will it survive a drop?

What does the work, in order

  1. The foam liner. It decouples the box from the case, so the box decelerates over 5 mm of crush instead of instantly — roughly 5–10× less peak force on the box. This is the single biggest item and the cheapest.
  2. Wall thickness. Impact resistance scales roughly with the cube of thickness. 5 mm → 8 mm is about 2.5× the energy before it cracks.
  3. Radius every internal corner, R2 minimum. A sharp internal corner in acrylic has a theoretically infinite stress concentration. A laser can cut any radius — so do it at every finger root, pocket rim and notch corner.
  4. Anneal after cutting. 2–3 hours at 80 °C, then cool slowly inside the oven. Laser cutting locks stress into the cut edge, and that stress is why acrylic shatters instead of flexing. Cheapest upgrade there is, and almost nobody does it.
  5. Polish the cut edges. A laser-cut edge is a row of micro-cracks. Flame polish or wet-sand to 1500 grit.
  6. Box-jointed corners. Nine interlocking fingers per corner spread the impact instead of failing along one line, and no glue means no weak link.
  7. A captive floor. The base plate is boxed in by the corner joinery rather than magnetically held, so a drop cannot pop it off. Only the lid is magnetic now.
  8. A flat underside. The walls run right down to the floor, so the case sits on a continuous rim instead of on four feet.

The honest part

The magnetic lid will pop off in a hard drop.

The A8 lid plate weighs 168 g. A 1 m drop onto concrete decelerates at roughly 500 g, so the lid sees about 84 kg of inertial force. Four magnet pairs hold about 1.4 kg. No practical magnet array holds that.

This is exactly why the foam matters. When the lid comes off, the foam cradle still holds the box. You lose a lid, not the contents.

If you truly need the lid to stay on through a drop it has to be mechanically retained — a tongue engaging a lip inside the walls — but then the case takes the whole impact and cracks instead. A lid that pops off is a lid that releases energy.

Test it rather than trusting the numbers. Drop a prototype 1 m onto concrete in all six orientations.

08Assembly, step by step

Eight steps. No glue, no fasteners, no tools beyond a drill for the eight magnet pockets. Two of the eight steps are the whole assembly — each front or back wall goes in with a single straight push, and both its corners engage in that one move.

1 · Lay out every part 9 acrylic panels, 16 magnets, 6 foam pads. Nothing is glued in the factory.
2 · Drill the 8 pockets One in each wall's top edge, four in the lid. The base needs none.
3 · Bond the wall magnets 4, one in each wall's top edge. Flush, not proud.
4 · Bond the lid magnets Let the wall magnets pull each one round the right way before you glue.
5 · Base plate in, side walls on The base slides in at the second finger band; its corner tabs drop into the sockets.
6 · Front wall in One push across its face: both corners mesh at once.
7 · Back wall in Same push the other way. All six faces are now connected.
8 · Foam, box, lid The lid is the only magnetically-held part of the case.
One thing a flush lid costs you. A perfectly flush lid has nothing to grip. The 20 × 4 mm scallop in the front wall's top rim is the one place the rectangle is not perfect — and it is the difference between a case that opens easily and one that generates support emails. Alternatively press firmly on one corner and the opposite corner levers up.
